## What It Is

Body Protection Compound-157 — a 15-amino-acid peptide (a lab-synthesized fragment originally isolated from human stomach juice, thought to mimic a natural healing signal your gut already makes)

## What It Does

Think of your body as a city, and BPC-157 as an emergency construction crew that rushes to wherever there's damage. It signals your cells to grow new blood vessels — like laying down new water pipes to a flooded neighborhood — so that oxygen and nutrients can reach injured tissue faster. It also dials down the alarm signals that cause swelling and redness after an injury, while activating specific repair pathways in muscle, tendon, and ligament cells. The result is that damaged tissue — whether it's a torn tendon, a gut ulcer, or a scraped wound — appears to heal faster and more completely in animal studies.

## Potential Benefits

- May speed up healing of tendons, ligaments, and muscles after injury, based on extensive animal research
- Could help protect and repair the stomach and intestinal lining — the same way a phone screen protector prevents cracks from spreading
- Research suggests it reduces inflammation, acting like a volume knob that turns down the body's overactive alarm system after injury
- May support recovery of nerve tissue, with animal studies showing improvements after spinal cord injuries
- Could help heal skin wounds, burns, and ulcers by promoting new tissue growth and blood vessel formation
- Research suggests it may protect the gut lining from damage caused by certain pain medications (like ibuprofen), essentially acting as a stomach shield

## Where It Came From

BPC-157 was first described in 1993 by Croatian pharmacologist Predrag Sikiric and his team at the University of Zagreb, who spent years collecting and analyzing human stomach juice to find a natural compound the body uses to protect itself from stress. Sikiric famously called his early search 'Substancija Boze Pomozi' — roughly 'substance God help me' — before settling on the name Body Protection Compound.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### 💉 SubQ Injection — Most Effective for Systemic/Orthopedic Use

Bypasses digestion entirely, delivering high bioavailability directly into circulation. Injecting near the injury site is described as concentrating the peptide locally in tendons, joints, ligaments, and muscle tissue. This is the gold-standard method used in virtually all preclinical studies.

### 💉 IM Injection — Effective for Deep, Large-Muscle Injuries

Similar bioavailability to SubQ but penetrates deeper tissue, making it useful for large-muscle injuries (e.g., quad tears, rotator cuff). Slightly more discomfort and technique-dependent; typically reserved for experienced users or clinical settings.

### 💊 Oral / Capsule — Most Effective for Gut-Specific Use

BPC-157 is unusually stable in gastric juice compared to other peptides, so it survives digestion better than most. When the goal is gut repair (leaky gut, IBD, ulcers), oral delivery acts locally along the GI lining — which is actually superior for that specific application. However, systemic absorption is lower, making it a poor choice for orthopedic or vascular healing. Enteric-coated capsules improve survival through the stomach.

### 👅 Sublingual — Middle Ground Between Oral and Injectable

Held under the tongue for 60–90 seconds, a fraction absorbs into the bloodstream through mucosal tissue, bypassing some digestion. Faster onset than swallowed capsules, but bioavailability still well below injection.

### 🩸 IV — Maximum Bioavailability, Clinical Setting Only

Used in some research/clinical settings (pilot studies at 10–20 mg in saline). Offers maximum bioavailability but impractical outside a supervised clinical environment; elimination half-life is very short (~15 min after IV), meaning the peptide clears quickly.

## Dosage Context

- Lower end of the range reported in practitioner protocols and research literature: 250 mcg administered per day via SubQ injection (or 250–500 mcg/day oral capsule for gut-focused use). This is a conservative starting point to assess tolerance and response before committing to full cycles.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 500 mcg administered per day via SubQ — the most commonly used amount across clinical practitioners and biohackers. For moderate injuries or gut repair, this is the standard working dose, split into AM + PM (250 mcg each) for sustained coverage.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: 750 mcg–1,000 mcg administered per day, often split into two injections (500 mcg AM + 250–500 mcg PM), used for post-surgical recovery or severe tissue trauma. Some practitioners use up to 1,000 mcg/day in acute post-surgical protocols. Beyond 1,000 mcg/day there is no well-documented benefit, and higher doses do not appear to scale linearly with results.
- Note: No established formal human clinical dosing exists — all figures are derived from animal-to-human dose conversions and practitioner/biohacker experience. A basic weight-based estimate is approximately 2.5–3.75 mcg per kilogram of body weight per dose, twice daily.

## Timing & Cycling

Frequency: Daily injections or oral doses are standard. Twice-daily split dosing (AM + PM) is preferred for post-surgical, high-trauma, or chronic injury protocols to maintain steady peptide levels.

Morning vs. night: For SubQ injection, once-daily users typically dose in the evening after training, allowing the peptide to work overnight when growth hormone is naturally elevated and tissue repair is most active. For split dosing, morning and evening are both used. For preventive/maintenance purposes, PM dosing is the most common choice.

Fasted vs. fed (oral): Oral BPC-157 should be taken on an empty stomach, or 30–60 minutes before a protein-based meal. Food — especially protein — triggers digestive enzymes that can break down the peptide before it reaches the intestinal lining. For injectable routes, food timing is irrelevant.

Workout timing: No strict peri-workout requirement. For injury recovery, consistency of daily dosing matters more than precise workout-relative timing. Some users report injecting post-training to address exercise-induced tissue stress, but there is no strong evidence that pre- vs. post-training injection changes outcomes meaningfully.

BPC-157 is cycled on and off — it is not intended for indefinite continuous use. The standard approach is 4–6 weeks on, followed by 4–8 weeks off before repeating. For more serious conditions (post-surgery, chronic joint degeneration), cycles can run 8–12 weeks, still followed by a meaningful break.

For maintenance or injury prevention, a lighter protocol of 250 mcg/day for 4–6 weeks every 3–4 months is commonly used.

Why cycle? There is no evidence of physical dependence or withdrawal, and no formal PCT (post-cycle therapy) is required. Cycling is recommended to prevent receptor desensitization, give the body time to consolidate repair, and avoid any unknown long-term effects from continuous use. The peptide's short half-life (under 30 minutes after injection) means it clears rapidly and does not accumulate between doses.

Important: BPC-157 is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
