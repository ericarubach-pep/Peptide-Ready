## What It Is

Cartalax (also designated AED or T-31) — a synthetic tripeptide (3-amino-acid chain: alanine-glutamic acid-aspartic acid) designed to support cartilage health and joint repair at the cellular level.

## What It Does

Cartilage is the rubbery tissue that cushions your joints — it's notoriously bad at repairing itself because it has no blood supply. Cartalax is a tiny 3-amino-acid peptide that gets inside cartilage cells (called chondrocytes) and interacts directly with DNA, acting like a small volume knob for specific genes. It turns up the genes responsible for building cartilage's structural matrix (the proteins and sugars that give cartilage its springy strength) and turns down the genes responsible for breaking it down and causing inflammation. It also suppresses signals that cause cartilage cells to age prematurely or die.

## Potential Benefits

- May support collagen and cartilage matrix production in joint tissue
- Could suppress the inflammatory enzymes (called MMPs) that eat away at cartilage in conditions like osteoarthritis
- Research suggests it reduces cartilage cell death under inflammatory conditions
- Russian observational studies report 20–40% pain reduction and 15–30% mobility improvement in osteoarthritis patients after 2–6 months
- Could slow cellular aging in cartilage tissue
- May improve antioxidant defenses in joint cells

## Where It Came From

Developed in Russia by Vladimir Khavinson and colleagues at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology as part of a broader research program studying small peptide regulators derived from specific body tissues. Development took place approximately in the 1990s to 2000s.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### 💉 Injection under the skin / subcutaneous (primary research route)

All experimental injection protocols use subcutaneous administration. The peptide is delivered near the bloodstream and distributed to cartilage and connective tissue targets. This is the route with the most practical protocol data available.

**💉 Intramuscular injection / into the muscle (used for joint health, some sources)**

Some protocols describe IM injection for cartilage repair and joint conditions, particularly when the target tissue is deep joint cartilage. Absorption is similar to subcutaneous.

### 💊 Oral capsule (available commercially)

Cartalax is available in oral capsule form (primarily from Eastern European markets where cytomedine peptides are more established). Bioavailability through the gut is lower than injection, but the oral form exists as a convenient alternative. One common commercial format is 2 capsules per day for 1–3 months.

### 🏥 IV (not established)

No IV protocol exists for Cartalax.

## Dosage Context

- Note: Cartalax has very limited published human clinical data. Its research base comes mainly from Eastern European animal and in vitro studies. No FDA-approved dosing guidelines exist.
- Lower end of the reported range: 1–2 mg subcutaneously once daily for 10–20 days (short course). This mirrors the lower-range research protocols.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 2–4 mg subcutaneously once daily over 8–12 weeks, with gradual titration starting at 2 mg/day and increasing by 0.5–1 mg every 1–2 weeks.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: Up to 5 mg subcutaneously once daily by weeks 7–12 of a protocol. Some formulation sources reference 2–5 mg/day as the working range.
- Oral capsule equivalent: 2 capsules per day for 1–3 months (per commercial product guidelines, though equivalent injectable doses are uncertain).
- For a 10-day acute course: 1–2 mg administered daily. For a longer 8–12 week tissue support protocol: 2–5 mg administered daily with gradual increase.

## Timing & Cycling

Once daily at a consistent time. There are no known interactions with food, hormones, or circadian rhythms that would make a specific time-of-day optimal. Morning dosing is practical and allows full daily activity before any local injection site reaction resolves. Injection sites are typically rotated between the abdomen, thighs, and upper arms.

Cartalax is designed for pulsed use, not continuous indefinite use. The typical model is a defined course (10–20 days for acute protocols, or 8–12 weeks for longer cartilage support protocols) followed by a rest period of 4–6 months before repeating if needed. Commercial oral products often specify 1–3 months of use followed by a repeat cycle 4–6 months later. This cycling approach reflects how cytomedine peptides (the class to which Cartalax belongs) are traditionally used — they work by temporarily shifting cellular signaling toward repair and homeostasis, and periodic courses are considered sufficient rather than continuous dosing.

Important: Cartalax is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
