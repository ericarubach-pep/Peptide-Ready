## What It Is

Glutathione (GSH) — a tripeptide (3 amino acids: glutamate, cysteine, and glycine) that acts as the body's master intracellular antioxidant, produced naturally by nearly every cell

## What It Does

Think of glutathione as your body's in-house cleanup and recycling crew, working inside virtually every cell around the clock. It directly neutralizes reactive oxygen species (free radicals) before they can damage DNA, proteins, and cell membranes — similar to a sponge soaking up spilled chemicals before they spread. It also acts as a "recharger" for other antioxidants, regenerating spent vitamin C and vitamin E so they can go back to work. In the liver, glutathione is a key cofactor for Phase II detoxification enzymes, grabbing onto toxins and heavy metals and tagging them so they can be flushed out through bile or urine — like an escort walking unwanted guests to the exit. Your body already produces roughly 10–14 grams of it daily, but production can be outpaced by stress, toxin exposure, illness, or aging, which is why supplementation is researched as a way to top up cellular reserves.

Glutathione is your cells' built-in rust-prevention system. It neutralizes harmful, unstable molecules called free radicals — the chemical byproducts of normal metabolism that can damage DNA and cell membranes if left unchecked. It also acts as the body's main detox agent, grabbing onto heavy metals, carcinogens, and other environmental toxins and escorting them out. On top of that, it helps keep your immune system sharp and recharges other antioxidants like vitamins C and E.

## Potential Benefits

### From the clinical-detail write-up

- Neutralizes free radicals and reduces oxidative stress markers, with liposomal oral dosing shown to lower plasma 8-isoprostane by about 35% in a pilot human trial
- Supports liver Phase II detoxification and heavy-metal clearance by conjugating toxins for excretion — a pilot study also reported benefit in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
- May enhance immune function, with clinical trials showing increased NK cell cytotoxicity and fewer cold symptoms in older adults taking oral glutathione for several months
- Recycles other antioxidants (vitamin C and vitamin E) back into their active forms, extending their protective effects
- IV protocols have shown modest reductions in melanin index (12–24% over 8–12 weeks) in randomized trials for melasma, though evidence for general skin-brightening claims beyond this specific use is much weaker and inconsistently regulated
- Small clinical pilot studies have explored IV glutathione for Parkinson's disease symptom support and for reducing chemotherapy-induced nerve pain (cisplatin neuropathy), though evidence remains preliminary

### From the plain-language write-up

- Provides powerful protection against cell damage caused by unstable molecules (free radicals)
- May strengthen immune function, including the activity of cells that hunt and destroy threats
- Could help detoxify harmful chemicals, environmental pollutants, and certain drugs
- Research suggests supplementation may raise blood glutathione levels by 30–35% over 6 months
- May support healthy mitochondrial function (the energy-generating parts of your cells)
- Could play a role in slowing some aspects of aging at the cellular level

## Where It Came From

Glutathione's discovery stretches back further than most peptides on this list. In 1888, French chemist Joseph de Rey-Pailhade isolated a sulfur-containing substance from yeast extract that he named "philothion" (Greek for "sulfur-loving"), though he lacked the tools to fully characterize it. The compound resurfaced in 1921, when Cambridge biochemist Frederick Gowland Hopkins extracted it from muscle tissue and gave it the name "glutathione," initially believing it was a two-amino-acid molecule. In 1929, researchers Hunter and Eagles determined it was actually a tripeptide of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine, a finding confirmed shortly after through crystallization. Cornell biochemist Vincent du Vigneaud helped elucidate its structure and synthesis in the mid-1930s, and it wasn't until the 1970s and 1980s — through the work of researchers like Helmut Sies — that glutathione's central role in defending cells against oxidative stress became clearly understood, cementing its reputation as the body's "master antioxidant."

First isolated in 1929 by Sir Frederick Gowland Hopkins at the University of Cambridge, UK, who found it in yeast and red blood cells — the same year he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### 💧 IV (Intravenous) — Highest Bioavailability, Clinic Setting

Delivers glutathione directly into the bloodstream, achieving close to 100% bioavailability and an immediate rise in plasma levels. This is the standard route used in most published clinical protocols for liver support, heavy-metal detox, and melasma trials. The catch: cellular uptake is rate-limited by transporter saturation, so a bigger dose doesn't always mean proportionally more antioxidant benefit at the cellular level, and effects fade within 2–4 hours as the liver clears the bolus.

### 💉 IM Injection — Strong Bioavailability, More Convenient Than IV

Bypasses digestion entirely and delivers roughly 80–90% bioavailability, with peak plasma levels 2–6 hours post-injection. It's the standard route for skin-radiance and general wellness protocols outside a clinic, offering a strong bioavailability-to-convenience trade-off compared to IV.

### 💊 Liposomal Oral — Best Non-Injectable Option

Encapsulates the glutathione molecule in a phospholipid shell that protects it from being broken down by gut enzymes (particularly gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase) before it can be absorbed. Clinical pilot studies report meaningful increases in whole blood, plasma, and immune cell glutathione levels within one to two weeks of daily liposomal dosing — a result standard oral capsules have failed to reliably produce. This is generally considered the most practical option for regular at-home use.

### 👅 Sublingual — Moderate Option, Bypasses First-Pass Metabolism

Reduced glutathione held under the tongue absorbs partly through the oral mucosa, sidestepping some digestive breakdown and first-pass liver metabolism. Bioavailability sits below liposomal oral and injection but above standard capsules, and effectiveness depends heavily on consistent hold time.

### 💊 Standard Oral (Non-Liposomal) — Least Effective

Plain glutathione capsules or tablets face a steep bioavailability problem: gut enzymes cleave the molecule's gamma-glutamyl bond, breaking it into its three amino acids before it reaches circulation intact. A 2014 randomized trial found a single 500 mg oral dose produced no measurable rise in plasma glutathione at 60, 90, or 120 minutes. Bioavailability is typically estimated at just 5–15%, and doses below 500 mg/day show negligible effect.

### 🌬️ Nebulized (Inhaled) — Niche, Lung-Specific Use

Occasionally used in respiratory-focused protocols to deliver glutathione directly to lung tissue. Research on this route is limited and it isn't used for general systemic antioxidant support — it's a specialized option rather than a mainstream one.

### Subcutaneous injection (from the plain-language write-up)

Slightly slower absorption than intramuscular delivery, but noted as practical for home use; common in at-home wellness programs described in the source material.

### Topical / on skin (from the plain-language write-up)

Skin creams containing glutathione are popular for skin-brightening use but do not meaningfully raise blood glutathione levels according to the source material; effects described as staying local to the skin.

## Dosage Context

- IV dose: 600–1,200 mg per session for general detox/wellness protocols; 1,400–2,400 mg per session for more intensive chronic-illness protocols (the reference Parkinson's pilot trial used 1,400 mg three times weekly for four weeks).
- IM injection dose: 200–600 mg per injection, 1–3 times per week, typically run in 8–12 week cycles (beginner ~200 mg 1–2x/week; advanced ~600 mg 2–3x/week).
- Liposomal oral dose: 500 mg once daily is the most common consumer dose and matches the two largest published human trials; some protocols use up to 500 mg twice daily (1,000 mg/day total) for a more aggressive antioxidant push.
- Sublingual dose: 100–250 mg, held under the tongue for 60–90 seconds, once or twice daily.
- Standard oral dose: Generally not recommended as a primary route given its low bioavailability; if used, doses of 500–1,000 mg/day are needed just to approach the effects seen with much lower liposomal doses.
- Note: There is no single FDA-established clinical dose. Ranges above are drawn from published pilot trials and clinical/practitioner protocols, not large-scale dose-finding studies.

### Additional dose points from the plain-language write-up

- Lower end of the reported range: 600 mg per injection (IM or IV), once per week. For oral/sublingual: 250–500 mg administered per day.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 600–1,200 mg per injection, 1–3 times per week (IM, IV, or subcutaneous). For skin brightening goals, some protocols use 1,200–1,500 mg per session, 2–3 times weekly. For daily subcutaneous home programs, 100–300 mg administered per day is a gentler maintenance range.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: 1,400–4,000 mg per session for intensive cosmetic or therapeutic protocols, always under medical supervision. Clinical studies for Parkinson's disease used 1,400 mg three times per week. Body weight does not typically dictate the dose in most wellness protocols, but metabolic demand (stress, illness, toxin exposure) does.

## Timing & Cycling

Frequency: Oral and liposomal forms are typically taken once or twice daily; IV and IM protocols are usually run 1–3 times per week during an active course, then tapered to monthly maintenance.

Morning vs. night: Morning dosing on an empty stomach is the most commonly recommended timing for oral, liposomal, and injectable glutathione, intended to support antioxidant activity throughout the day. There's no strong evidence that evening dosing is inferior.

Fasted vs. fed: Oral and liposomal capsules are generally taken 30–60 minutes before food for better absorption, since food can compete for uptake pathways.

Cofactor pairing: Glutathione is frequently paired with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), alpha-lipoic acid, selenium, or vitamin C, which support the enzymes that recycle used (oxidized) glutathione back into its active form. If injecting, vitamin C should be given as a separate dose rather than mixed in the same syringe, as combining them can cause precipitation.

Morning injections are preferred for detox and energy-focused goals because they align with the liver's natural peak detoxification activity and the day's cortisol rhythm. Evening injections are preferred by those focused on skin repair or recovery, as the body does much of its cellular repair at night. For IV or IM protocols done at a clinic, timing is often dictated by appointment availability. For daily subcutaneous programs, consistency matters more than the specific time — a consistent time each day is generally favored over precise clock timing daily. Glutathione can be taken with or without food.

Unlike many synthetic peptides, glutathione is a molecule the body already produces continuously, so it doesn't carry the same cycling requirements as a hormone-signaling peptide. That said, some cycling is increasingly recommended rather than optional: one study found that continuous supplementation at 500 mg/day for eight weeks reduced the body's own glutathione-synthesizing enzyme activity (GCLC/GCLM) by 25–45%, suggesting cells scale back native production when external supply is abundant.

Common approach: A loading phase of daily (oral/liposomal) or 2–3x weekly (IV/IM) dosing for 4–8 weeks, followed by either a maintenance phase (once weekly or a lower daily dose) or a short break of several weeks before resuming — often paired with NAC, alpha-lipoic acid, or selenium to keep the body's own recycling machinery engaged during the cycle.

Why cycle: The goal is to avoid long-term downregulation of the body's endogenous production pathway while still getting the benefit of elevated glutathione during periods of higher oxidative stress, toxin exposure, or targeted use (like a skin-brightening or detox protocol). For general low-dose daily wellness use, many people do take liposomal glutathione continuously without a formal break, since it's a naturally occurring molecule rather than a synthetic signaling peptide.

Important: High-dose IV glutathione for off-label uses (such as skin lightening) carries documented risks including allergic reactions and has drawn regulatory warnings in some countries. Glutathione is not FDA-approved for most of the wellness uses described above. Physician consultation before use in any form, and especially by injection, is the standard recommendation across these source materials.

Most clinical wellness protocols follow a two-phase structure: a loading phase (1–3 sessions per week for 4–8 weeks) to build up reserves, followed by a maintenance phase (1–2 sessions per month) to sustain levels. Some individuals with chronic conditions use continuous weekly dosing indefinitely under medical supervision. Pure oral supplementation can be taken daily long-term without cycling concerns. For injectable protocols, some practitioners recommend brief breaks (1–2 weeks off every 2–3 months) to let the body's natural synthesis recalibrate, though there is no strong clinical evidence making this mandatory.
