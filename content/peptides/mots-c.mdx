## What It Is

Mitochondrial Open Reading Frame of the 12S rRNA-c — a 16-amino-acid peptide (a lab-made version of a tiny protein your own mitochondria — the power plants inside every cell — naturally produce and release as a distress signal when your metabolism is under stress)

## What It Does

Your cells are like tiny factories, and the mitochondria are the power generators humming in the back room. MOTS-c is like a text message that the generators send out when fuel is running low: 'Hey nucleus, we need help — switch the factory into energy-saving mode.' It travels from the mitochondria all the way to the cell's control center (the nucleus) and flips a set of genetic switches that tell your muscles to absorb sugar more efficiently. It also activates a master energy sensor called AMPK — imagine a circuit breaker that kicks in when your body is burning too much power — which helps prevent fat from building up and keeps blood sugar in check.

## Potential Benefits

- May improve the body's ability to respond to insulin, meaning cells become better at absorbing sugar from the blood rather than leaving it floating around
- Research suggests it could prevent or reduce obesity in mice fed a high-fat diet, without reducing how much they eat
- Could help fight age-related decline in physical performance — studies show it significantly boosted treadmill endurance in mice of all ages, including elderly mice
- May reduce harmful inflammation linked to metabolic stress and aging, acting like a cooling system for an overheating engine
- Research suggests it supports muscle health by helping cells handle energy more efficiently during exercise
- Could have protective effects on the heart and liver in metabolic disease, with animal studies showing reduced damage in diabetic models

## Where It Came From

MOTS-c was discovered in 2015 by Changhan Lee, Pinchas Cohen, and colleagues at the University of Southern California's Leonard Davis School of Gerontology in Los Angeles, USA. It was one of the first peptides ever found to be encoded not in the main DNA blueprint most people know about, but in the separate, ancient genome that lives inside our mitochondria — a surprise that rewrote what scientists thought mitochondria could do.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### SubQ Injection (most effective and most common)

MOTS-c is a mitochondria-derived peptide that acts systemically on metabolic pathways (AMPK activation, insulin signaling, fat oxidation). SubQ injection delivers it directly into circulation, bypassing the gut and ensuring reliable bioavailability. This is the route used in virtually all practitioner protocols and the one supported by the closest analog human trial (CB-4211 study: 25 mg subQ daily). Isotonic bacteriostatic water (with sodium chloride) is used for reconstitution in these protocols — standard BAC water alone can cause painful injection-site reactions with MOTS-c.

### IM Injection

Functionally similar to SubQ for systemic peptides; some practitioners use IM for slightly faster absorption into circulation. Produces the same metabolic effects but adds discomfort without a proven advantage over SubQ. Less commonly used.

### Oral (experimental/emerging)

An advanced oral formulation of MOTS-c exists and is being explored (as noted in the context of CB-4211), but human data are extremely limited. Oral peptides are generally degraded by digestive enzymes, and without enteric protection or special formulations, oral MOTS-c is not considered a reliable route for most users. Not currently viewed as a reliable substitute for injection.

## Dosage Context

- Note: There is NO formally established human dosing for MOTS-c. All figures below are extrapolated from animal studies (dose conversion from mouse intraperitoneal to human SubQ) and practitioner/biohacker protocols. Treat all numbers as informed estimates, not clinical standards.
- Lower end of the range reported in practitioner protocols and research literature: 5 mg per injection, 2–3 times per week (approximately 10–15 mg total per week). This is the most widely recommended starting point. Some cautious practitioners begin at 2–3 mg per injection for very sensitive individuals or those with MTHFR methylation variants.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 5–10 mg per injection, 2–3 times per week (10–30 mg/week total). The most commonly cited practitioner protocol is 5 mg three times per week. One influential calculation based on mouse data converts to approximately 3.3 mg/day (~23 mg/week) for a 70 kg adult, aligning with 7.5 mg three times per week at the higher end.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: 10 mg per injection, 3–7 times per week (up to 30–70 mg/week). The Beverly Hills Rejuvenation Center lists 10 mg per injection as their standard therapeutic dose. The one human analog trial (CB-4211) used 25 mg/day SubQ for 4 weeks without significant adverse events. Higher frequency (daily) dosing is used in some longevity-focused clinical protocols but lacks independent safety data.
- Note: Weight-based dosing is sometimes referenced: roughly 0.048 mg/kg/day as a human equivalent of the most-studied mouse dose.

## Timing & Cycling

Frequency: 2–3 injections per week is the standard protocol, though some practitioners use once-weekly (e.g., Ben Greenfield: 10 mg once weekly) or near-daily dosing for aggressive longevity protocols.

Morning vs. night: Morning is strongly preferred. MOTS-c acts acutely over hours — not gradually over weeks — so timing the injection to coincide with peak metabolic and exercise activity windows is important. Morning dosing also aligns with natural circadian metabolic rhythms and allows users to benefit from the energy-boosting effects throughout the day rather than disrupting sleep. Some users who experience energy crashes dose in the evening instead, but this is less common.

Fasted vs. fed: Fasted morning is the preferred state, on an empty stomach before eating.

Workout timing: Dosing 30–60 minutes before aerobic exercise (Zone 2 cardio specifically) is a commonly cited approach. MOTS-c has synergistic effects with aerobic exercise — it activates the same AMPK pathways that endurance training activates, effectively amplifying the metabolic signal. Timing the dose before Zone 2 cardio is a widely cited optimization. On non-training days, morning fasted dosing is still recommended to maintain metabolic signaling.

MOTS-c is cycled on and off — continuous indefinite use is not currently supported by evidence and is not recommended by most practitioners.

Standard cycle: 4–6 weeks on, followed by 2–4 weeks off. Some practitioners use a less frequent dosing approach (once weekly) to allow longer total use periods of up to 8–10 weeks before a break.

Why cycle? The cycling pattern is designed to preserve responsiveness to the peptide and mimic how endogenous MOTS-c behaves in the body — it naturally rises and falls with exercise rather than remaining constantly elevated. Continuous high-level signaling may theoretically blunt the AMPK response over time. Additionally, with no long-term human safety data, cycling limits cumulative exposure to an understudied compound.

Multiple cycles per year are common: users typically run 2–3 cycles annually, with at least 2–4 weeks off between each.

Important: MOTS-c is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
