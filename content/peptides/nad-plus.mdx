## What It Is

NAD+ (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide) — not technically a peptide, but a crucial molecule found in every cell of your body that acts as an essential helper for energy production and cellular repair. Supplements called NMN and NR are the most common ways to boost NAD+ levels.

## What It Does

Think of NAD+ as the rechargeable battery pack inside every cell. It shuttles energy from the food you eat into a form your cells can actually use (a molecule called ATP). But it also does something equally important: it powers a family of protective proteins called sirtuins that repair damaged DNA, regulate genes, and keep your mitochondria (the power plants inside your cells) healthy. The problem is NAD+ levels drop by roughly half between your 20s and 50s, largely because aging ramps up an enzyme called CD38 that constantly chews through your NAD+ supply — this decline is linked to nearly every hallmark of aging.

## Potential Benefits

- Research suggests restoring NAD+ can reverse several age-related declines in heart, muscle, and brain function in animals
- May reduce blood pressure and arterial stiffness based on small human trials
- Could improve how mitochondria work in people with heart failure
- Early human research suggests potential benefits in Parkinson's disease and poor circulation
- Supports your cells' ability to repair damaged DNA
- NMN and NR supplements have been shown to safely raise NAD+ levels in humans

## Where It Came From

NAD+ was first discovered in 1906 by Arthur Harden and William John Young in the UK while studying yeast fermentation. Hans von Euler-Chelpin won the 1929 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for working out its chemical structure. The connection between falling NAD+ levels and aging was pioneered by David Sinclair's lab at Harvard and Johan Auwerx at EPFL (Switzerland) starting around 2013.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### 🏥 Intravenous / IV infusion (most complete delivery)

When NAD+ is dripped directly into a vein, essentially 100% of the dose reaches the bloodstream with no losses. This is the highest-impact single-session approach. However, NAD+ is a large molecule that cannot cross cell membranes intact, so IV raises blood levels but intracellular levels may not rise proportionally. Sessions require 1–4 hours in a clinical setting.

**💉 Intramuscular injection / into the muscle (fast, moderate delivery)**

A smaller dose injected into muscle tissue absorbs into the bloodstream quickly. Does not require the slow drip rate of IV and can be done in minutes. Typical clinical doses are 100–200 mg per shot.

**💉 Injection under the skin / subcutaneous (convenient, gradual delivery)**

Absorbed more slowly than IM but well-suited for frequent at-home use. Delivers smaller, more frequent amounts to maintain stable blood levels.

### 💊 Oral supplements

NMN or NR (precursors that your body converts to NAD+) (moderate effectiveness) — The body converts these precursor molecules into NAD+ inside cells, which may actually be more effective at raising intracellular NAD+ than direct NAD+ injections. Oral NAD+ itself has poor absorption, but precursors are reasonably well absorbed. This is the most practical and well-evidenced long-term approach.

### 👃 Nasal spray (low-moderate, convenience-focused)

Some absorption through the nasal lining, useful for daily maintenance between injection sessions. Bioavailability is lower than injections.

### 🧴 Transdermal patch (low, variable)

Delivers NAD+ through the skin slowly, but absorption is inconsistent and depends on individual skin type and placement.

## Dosage Context

- IV infusion:
- Beginner: 100–250 mg per session (first-time patients, allows body to adjust, shorter infusion time).
- Typical: 250–500 mg per session, 1–3 times per week during a loading phase, then monthly for maintenance.
- High/advanced: 750–1,000 mg per session (requires 4–6 hour slow drip; used for specific clinical indications under physician supervision).
- Subcutaneous injection:
- Beginner: 30–50 mg per injection, daily for at least 1 week.
- Typical: 50–150 mg per injection, 2–3 times per week.
- High: 100–200 mg per injection, daily during a loading phase (first 7–14 days).
- Oral precursors (NMN or NR):
- Beginner: 250 mg administered daily.
- Typical: 500–900 mg administered daily (well-studied range showing measurable NAD+ increases).
- High: Up to 1,200–2,000 mg administered daily (used in some studies, generally well tolerated).

## Timing & Cycling

Oral doses are commonly split between morning and evening — the morning dose is described as supporting daytime energy production, and the evening dose as supporting overnight cellular repair. When a mild energizing effect is noticed, an earlier evening dose (e.g., 6 PM rather than bedtime) is a commonly reported adjustment. Injections are typically done 2–3 times per week; consistency of schedule matters more than specific time of day. IV sessions are clinic-based and scheduled per provider protocol. There are no strict food restrictions, though taking oral precursors with a small amount of food may reduce stomach discomfort.

Oral NAD+ precursor supplements are generally taken daily and continuously — levels need consistent replenishment to stay in the optimal range, and stopping allows them to drift back down. IV therapy follows a loading phase (2–4 weeks of more frequent sessions) followed by a maintenance phase (monthly sessions). Subcutaneous injections similarly begin with a loading phase then taper to a maintenance schedule. Unlike some peptides that require cycling to prevent receptor desensitization, NAD+ supplementation does not appear to cause tolerance — it simply needs to be maintained consistently. Long-term continuous use is the norm.

Important: NAD+ is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
