## What It Is

A naturally occurring amino-acid compound that recharges the body's fastest energy system — one of the most-studied and most effective supplements in existence

## What It Does

Creatine is a small nitrogen-containing compound made in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas from three amino acids, and stored mostly in skeletal muscle (about 95%) with the rest in the brain and other tissues. Its job is to recycle ATP, the cell's basic energy currency. During short, intense efforts — a heavy lift, a sprint, a jump — muscle burns through ATP faster than it can be regenerated through normal metabolism. Phosphocreatine, the stored form of creatine, donates a phosphate group back to spent ADP almost instantly, regenerating ATP and letting the muscle keep producing force for a few extra seconds or reps.

Think of it as a battery pack sitting right next to the engine, ready to hand over a fast burst of charge the moment demand spikes, rather than waiting for the slower supply chain to catch up. Supplementing with creatine increases the size of that battery pack — raising muscle phosphocreatine and free creatine stores by roughly 20 to 40% above baseline — which is why it reliably improves performance in repeated high-intensity efforts even though it doesn't function anything like a stimulant or hormone.

## Potential Benefits

- Increased strength and power output — one of the most consistently replicated effects in all of sports nutrition, across more than 1,000 published studies
- Greater lean muscle mass — partly from enhanced training capacity and partly from water drawn into muscle cells, a documented and harmless effect
- Improved high-intensity exercise capacity — better performance in repeated sprints, sets, and other short, explosive efforts lasting under ~30 seconds
- Faster recovery between sets — reduced accumulation of lactate, ammonia, and other fatigue-related byproducts during intermittent high-intensity work
- Cognitive support — modest but growing evidence for improved memory and working memory, particularly under conditions of brain energy stress (sleep deprivation, aging, mild traumatic brain injury)
- Bone and muscle support in older adults — encouraging data on muscle mass and strength preservation when combined with resistance training
- Therapeutic interest in neuromuscular and neurological conditions — studied as an adjunct for muscular dystrophy, depression, and recovery from concussion, though most of this research is still early-stage

## Where It Came From

Creatine's story starts in a beaker of meat broth, not a gym. In 1832, French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul isolated a new crystalline compound from an extract of skeletal muscle and named it after the Greek word for flesh, kreas. German chemist Justus von Liebig confirmed creatine's presence in animal muscle in 1847 and noticed something telling: wild foxes carried roughly ten times more muscle creatine than domesticated ones, an early hint that creatine content tracked with physical activity.

In 1912, Harvard researchers Otto Folin and Willey Glover Denis showed that ingesting creatine could dramatically raise the creatine content of muscle tissue, establishing that the body could absorb and store supplemental creatine rather than simply excreting it. Alfred Chanutin ran one of the first human supplementation experiments in 1926. But it was a landmark 1992 study by Roger Harris and colleagues that defined the modern era: it established the classic loading-and-maintenance dosing protocol still used today and helped trigger the explosion of creatine research and commercial use that followed throughout the 1990s.

## How It's Taken

Creatine is a different story from most compounds in this series: plain creatine monohydrate powder already achieves close to 99% oral bioavailability, so there isn't a poorly-absorbed "problem" for fancier formulations to solve the way there is with curcumin or glutathione. The differences between the forms below come down to convenience, mixability, GI comfort, and formulation stability rather than dramatically different amounts of creatine reaching your muscles.

### 📊 Creatine Monohydrate Powder — The Gold Standard

Plain creatine monohydrate is the form used in the vast majority of the 1,000+ studies behind creatine's safety and performance data, and remains the benchmark every other form is measured against. It is stable, inexpensive, and reaches close to complete intestinal absorption at standard doses. Micronized versions (finer particle size) dissolve faster and feel less gritty in water but offer no additional performance benefit over standard-grade monohydrate — the ergogenic effect plateaus once muscle stores are saturated, regardless of particle size.

### 💧 Liposomal Liquid — Best Convenience & Stability Option

Liposomal creatine encapsulates the molecule in phospholipid vesicles, protecting it from degradation and allowing it to be sold as a ready-to-drink liquid rather than a powder you mix yourself. This solves a real problem: older, non-encapsulated liquid creatine products degrade into creatinine (an inactive breakdown product) once dissolved in water for any length of time, which is why plain pre-mixed liquid creatine has historically underperformed powder in head-to-head testing. Early data on enhanced liposomal-style formulations shows meaningfully higher blood exposure (AUC) and peak concentration compared to standard micronized creatine, though rigorous, independent, peer-reviewed trials on true liposomal creatine specifically are still limited. In practice, this route is best thought of as a strong choice for people who want a pre-dosed, no-mixing, gut-friendly option — not necessarily a category upgrade in raw effectiveness.

### 💊 Creatine Hydrochloride (HCl) Capsules

Creatine HCl binds creatine to hydrochloric acid, making it roughly 38 times more soluble in water than monohydrate. Marketing around this form claims better absorption and smaller effective doses, but head-to-head trials have consistently found no meaningful advantage over monohydrate for strength, muscle size, or performance once total creatine intake is matched. The higher solubility is real; the bioavailability leap is not well substantiated. It's a reasonable option for people who dislike monohydrate's grittiness, but it commands a significant price premium for a benefit that hasn't been clearly demonstrated.

### 🚰 Traditional Pre-Mixed Liquid (Non-Liposomal)

Standard bottled liquid creatine that isn't stabilized through liposomal or similar encapsulation is the least reliable route. Creatine in solution slowly converts to creatinine over time, especially with heat or acidic exposure, meaning a portion of the labeled dose may already be inactive by the time it's consumed. Independent testing has generally found this format less effective than monohydrate powder, which is why it has fallen out of favor compared to newer liposomal liquid formats.

## Dosage Context

- Loading phase (optional): 20 g/day split into four 5 g doses for 5–7 days — saturates muscle stores within about a week
- Maintenance dose (most common approach): 3–5 g/day, taken daily including rest days — reaches full saturation in 3–4 weeks without loading
- Weight-based dosing: roughly 0.03 g/kg/day for maintenance, or 0.3 g/kg/day for a loading phase, used in some clinical protocols
- Liposomal liquid products: manufacturers commonly recommend 2–5 g/day depending on formulation strength; check the label, since concentration per serving varies more than with standard powder
- High-dose clinical/research range: up to 20–30 g/day has been studied short-term in neurological and neuromuscular disease populations under medical supervision — well above standard supplement use and not necessary for general training goals
- Note: muscle creatine storage has a hard ceiling (roughly 150–160 mmol/kg dry muscle weight); doses beyond what's needed to reach and maintain that ceiling provide no additional performance benefit

## Timing & Cycling

Unlike stimulants, creatine works by building up a saturated reserve over time rather than producing an acute effect, so the exact time of day matters far less than daily consistency. Some evidence suggests taking it with a meal containing carbohydrates and protein may modestly improve muscle uptake via insulin-mediated transport, which is why post-workout dosing alongside a meal is a common practice. But the most important factor by far is simply taking it every day, including rest days, since muscle stores are maintained through consistent daily intake rather than pre- or post-workout timing.

Is creatine cycled on and off? No -- this is one of the most thoroughly settled questions in sports nutrition: the International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand states there is no scientific evidence supporting cycling creatine, and long-term studies out to five years and doses up to 30 g/day have found no evidence of tolerance, downregulated natural production, or kidney or liver harm in healthy individuals. Unlike hormonal or receptor-based compounds, creatine has no feedback loop to desensitize — muscle stores simply decline gradually (roughly 1–2% per day) if use stops, and refill again once use resumes. Continuous daily use is the evidence-based standard. The main exceptions where structured breaks make sense are pre-competition water-weight management in some sports, or medical situations involving impaired kidney function, where a healthcare provider should guide use.
