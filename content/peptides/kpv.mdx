## What It Is

KPV (Lysine-Proline-Valine) — a 3-amino-acid peptide that is the active tail-end fragment of a natural hormone your skin and immune system already produce (called alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone)

## What It Does

KPV works primarily in the gut, where it gets absorbed through a dedicated transport channel in the intestinal lining. Once inside cells, it blocks the activation of a key inflammatory 'alarm switch' (called NF-kB) that would otherwise send out a cascade of distress signals causing swelling, redness, and tissue damage. It also helps reinforce the gut's protective barrier, keeping harmful substances from leaking into the bloodstream.

## Potential Benefits

- May reduce gut inflammation, with research showing it suppresses inflammatory bowel disease in animal models
- Could help strengthen the intestinal lining, relevant to conditions like 'leaky gut'
- Research suggests anti-inflammatory potency comparable to the full-length hormone it comes from
- May reduce skin inflammation, including contact-related rashes
- Could help prevent inflammation-driven colon cancer progression
- Research suggests antimicrobial properties against certain fungal infections

## Where It Came From

Identified in the 1990s–2000s by Thomas Luger and colleagues at the University of Münster, Germany, through systematic studies breaking apart the alpha-MSH hormone piece by piece to find which fragment was responsible for its anti-inflammatory effects.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

**💉 Injection under the skin / subcutaneous (most effective for systemic effects)**

Delivers KPV directly into the bloodstream for the fastest and most reliable systemic anti-inflammatory action. The go-to route for whole-body inflammatory conditions.

**💉 Injection into muscle / intramuscular (similarly effective, used interchangeably)**

Also used for systemic delivery; some protocols use IM, especially at higher doses.

### 💊 Oral / by mouth (effective specifically for gut conditions)

KPV is unusual among peptides in that it appears to survive digestion better than most, possibly because of an affinity for a gut transporter protein called hPepT1. This makes oral dosing genuinely useful for gut-specific inflammation (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, colitis). For systemic effects, injection is still preferred.

### 🧴 Topical / applied to skin (effective for local skin conditions)

Applied as creams or gels directly to inflamed or irritated skin. Works well for dermatitis, eczema, and wound healing locally. Does not produce meaningful systemic effects via this route.

### 👃 Nasal spray (under investigation, limited current data)

Being explored for neurological or sinus-related inflammatory applications but lacks established protocols.

## Dosage Context

- Lower end of the reported range: 200 mcg administered per day subcutaneously, starting in week 1. A gradual ramp is standard: 200 mcg/day for week 1, increasing to 300 mcg/day in week 2, 400 mcg/day in week 3, then 500 mcg/day from week 4 onward.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 200–500 mcg per injection, 1–2 times daily. The most commonly cited range in research-oriented protocols is 500 mcg once or twice daily.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: Some sources list doses of 0.5–2 mg per injection for more intensive applications (e.g., acute gastrointestinal inflammation). Oral supplementation for gut-specific use typically falls in the 1–5 mg administered per day range, as higher amounts are needed to compensate for partial digestive breakdown. Body weight does not usually dictate KPV dosing in standard protocols.

## Timing & Cycling

KPV is typically dosed 1–2 times daily. For injectable protocols, splitting the dose morning and evening helps maintain consistent anti-inflammatory levels throughout the day. Oral KPV (for gut use) is often taken with meals, since it needs to be present in the gut while digestion is active. There is no strong evidence linking workout timing to KPV effectiveness. For skin applications, topical KPV can be applied any time, though post-cleansing application (morning or evening skincare routine) is practical.

Most protocols run KPV in cycles of 4–8 weeks, followed by a break. For acute conditions (injury, flare-up), the cycle ends when the condition resolves. For chronic inflammatory conditions, longer courses of 8–12 weeks are used with periodic reassessment. Some practitioners support continuous low-dose use for chronic conditions under medical supervision, but the typical community approach is cyclic. Unlike GLP-1 drugs, KPV is not designed as a lifelong daily medication and is generally used in targeted courses.

Important: KPV is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
