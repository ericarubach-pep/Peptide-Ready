## What It Is

Kisspeptin — a family of peptides ranging from 10 to 54 amino acids long, all produced from the same gene (KISS1), that act as the master "on switch" for the reproductive system. The 54-amino-acid version is also called metastin.

## What It Does

Deep in your brain (in a region called the hypothalamus) there are neurons that control whether your reproductive system is switched on or off. Kisspeptin is the molecule that fires up those neurons. When kisspeptin locks onto its receiving station (called KISS1R) on these brain cells, it triggers a chain reaction: the hypothalamus releases a hormone that signals the pituitary gland to release two other hormones (LH and FSH), which then tell the ovaries or testes to produce estrogen or testosterone. In short, kisspeptin is the signal that starts the whole hormonal cascade of human reproduction and puberty.

## Potential Benefits

- May restore normal hormone cycles and fertility in people whose reproductive system has shut down due to low body weight, extreme stress, or other causes
- Could help trigger egg maturation in IVF procedures as a safer alternative to the standard hormone injection — live births have been reported in clinical trials
- Research suggests it could treat hormonal infertility in both men and women
- May influence mood, sexual motivation, and emotional processing
- Originally discovered as a cancer metastasis-suppressor — research suggests it may help prevent melanoma and breast cancer from spreading
- Could be used to block early puberty (using a blocker version of the drug)

## Where It Came From

The KISS1 gene was first identified in 1996 by Danny Welch's group at Pennsylvania State University, discovered accidentally while studying melanoma — and named after Hershey Kisses chocolates. Its role in controlling reproduction was uncovered in 2003 independently by Nicholas de Roux in France and Stephanie Seminara at Harvard, who found that people born without a working kisspeptin receptor never go through puberty.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

**🏥 Intravenous / IV bolus or infusion (most potent and studied route)**

Most published clinical studies used IV administration, especially for reproductive research. An IV bolus produces peak blood levels within minutes and reliably triggers the hormonal cascade (LH and FSH release). This route is primarily a research and clinical tool, not for self-use.

**💉 Injection under the skin / subcutaneous (practical clinical and research use)**

Subcutaneous injection is the practical route for ongoing protocols (e.g., supporting testosterone production during TRT). The typical dose is 100 mcg administered daily, or weight-based doses 2–3 times per week. Absorption is good and this route is suitable for self-administration.

### 🏥 Intravenous infusion (continuous, research only)

Continuous IV infusion at 1 nmol/kg/hour has been used in research to maintain steady LH stimulation. Not for self-use.

## Dosage Context

- Note: There is no FDA-approved dosing protocol for outpatient Kisspeptin use. All dosing is physician-guided.
- Lower end of the reported range: 50–100 mcg subcutaneously, 2–3 times per week. Used for supporting the hormone axis during testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) or for general hormone balance.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 100 mcg subcutaneously once daily, or weight-based dosing of 10–20 mcg per kg body weight given 2–3 times per week for TRT support. For fertility applications, 6.4 mcg/kg is used as a single injection to trigger ovulation.
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols (clinical context): 32 nmol/kg subcutaneously, or 10 nmol/kg IV bolus, used in specialized reproductive medicine settings. These are highly specific research/clinical doses requiring medical supervision.
- For a typical 80 kg person, 10 mcg/kg = approximately 800 mcg per dose for clinical reproductive protocols. The simpler TRT-support protocol uses a flat 100 mcg dose.

## Timing & Cycling

For TRT support: administer subcutaneously on the same days as testosterone injections, or 2–3 times per week on a consistent schedule. There is no strict food requirement. For ovulation induction in women: timing is critically dependent on the phase of the menstrual cycle — administration must occur during the preovulatory phase to be effective. During other phases of the cycle, Kisspeptin does not reliably stimulate hormone release. In men there is no cycle-dependent restriction.

Kisspeptin should not be used continuously at high frequencies due to the risk of receptor desensitization — if the receptor is stimulated constantly, it stops responding (this is called tachyphylaxis). In research settings, repeated boluses at 75-minute intervals maintained response in men, but daily high-dose use is generally avoided. For TRT support, protocols are typically run in parallel with testosterone therapy cycles. For fertility use, protocols are short and targeted (days to weeks). Practitioners generally recommend intermittent use or 4–8 week cycles with breaks, rather than indefinite continuous daily dosing.

Important: Kisspeptin is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
