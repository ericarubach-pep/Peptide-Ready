## What It Is

DSIP (Delta Sleep-Inducing Peptide) — a 9-amino-acid peptide found naturally in the brain's hypothalamus and bloodstream that, as the name suggests, was discovered by its ability to promote deep, slow-wave sleep.

## What It Does

DSIP appears to work on multiple systems at once. It interacts with the brain's chemical messaging systems — including calming receptors (similar to those targeted by anti-anxiety drugs) and stress-signaling pathways — to encourage the brain to shift into deep, restorative sleep. It also acts on the body's main stress-response system (involving the hormone cortisol) to reduce the stress response, and it's been shown to boost how efficiently your cells' power-generating machinery (mitochondria) converts fuel into usable energy.

## Potential Benefits

- May promote deeper, more restorative sleep and help people fall asleep faster
- Could reduce cortisol (stress hormone) levels and buffer the body's response to stress
- Research suggests mild pain-relieving properties
- Animal studies show a roughly 2.6-fold reduction in spontaneous tumor development
- May have neuroprotective and anti-aging effects based on animal research
- One clinical study reported relief of withdrawal symptoms in about 97% of opiate-dependent patients
- Early evidence for potential benefits in insomnia, chronic pain, and depression

## Where It Came From

First isolated in 1974 by Marcel Monnier and colleagues at the University of Basel in Switzerland. The discovery was made by drawing blood from rabbits while they were in an electrically induced state of deep sleep — and finding that something in that blood could transfer the sleep state to other animals.

## How It's Taken

Here's a breakdown of the available delivery methods and how they compare:

### 🏥 Intravenous / IV infusion (most studied and reliable route)

All key clinical sleep studies used slow IV infusion at a dose of 25 nmol/kg. This is what the published data supporting DSIP's sleep effects is actually based on. IV reliably delivers the full dose to the bloodstream. However, this requires a clinical setting and is not for self-use.

### 💉 Injection under the skin / subcutaneous (practical at-home route)

The most common self-administration route used in real-world peptide protocols. Absorption is somewhat less consistent than IV, and the evidence for this specific route is largely based on practitioner experience and extrapolation from IV studies rather than direct subcutaneous trials. Still considered effective in practice.

### 👃 Intranasal / nasal spray (moderate convenience)

A less invasive option. Some absorption occurs through the nasal lining, which connects to brain tissue more directly than systemic routes (potentially beneficial for a sleep peptide). Less studied for DSIP than injection routes.

### 💊 Sublingual tablet (emerging, variable)

Placed under the tongue for absorption through the mouth lining. Convenient but bioavailability is variable and less studied.

## Dosage Context

- Note: DSIP dosing is not FDA-approved. Clinical IV studies used weight-based dosing; subcutaneous protocols are extrapolated.
- Lower end of the reported range: 100–150 mcg subcutaneously, 30–60 minutes before bed. This cautious starting point suits people who are sensitive to neuroactive peptides.
- Typical range reported in the literature: 200–250 mcg subcutaneously, 1–3 hours before bed. This is the most widely used practitioner protocol. (For reference, the clinical IV dose for an 80 kg person equates to roughly 190–210 mcg, so 200–250 mcg subcutaneous is roughly analogous.)
- Higher end of the range documented in protocols: Up to 400 mcg subcutaneously. This is the upper end of community protocols. Higher doses increase the risk of next-day fatigue and may paradoxically disrupt natural sleep architecture rather than improve it.
- Research used IV doses daily for 4–7 consecutive nights. Some research suggests a single dose before bed improves sleep for several nights afterward, allowing less-frequent dosing.

## Timing & Cycling

Administration 30 minutes to 3 hours before intended bedtime is the typical approach. This timing aligns with DSIP's mechanism — it promotes the body's natural transition into deep sleep rather than sedating directly. Unlike a sleeping pill, DSIP works with the body's sleep architecture. One study found that a morning IV dose created sleep pressure within 130 minutes and improved the following night's sleep, suggesting that the exact nighttime timing requirement may be flexible — though most practitioners use pre-bedtime dosing for practical alignment. DSIP does not impair function during the day when properly timed.

Whether DSIP requires cycling is unclear from the research — one key source suggests it does not induce tolerance, but this is not the consensus view. Out of caution, most practitioners cycle it: a common protocol is 8 weeks on (5 nights per week) / 8 weeks off. Shorter acute courses of 4–10 days are also used, especially for short-term sleep disruption or stress events. Given the limited modern RCT data, erring on the side of cycling is prudent. Unlike the GLP-1 drugs, there is no evidence that stopping causes a rebound of worsened sleep.

Important: DSIP is not FDA-approved for human use as a research peptide and remains under investigation. All human use is off-label. Physician consultation before use in any form is the standard recommendation across these source materials.
